Holdout-Selected Histogram Boosting: A Budget-Aware Tabular Learner

ABench reference submissionStandard reference for task statement standard v2.1
Abstract

This submission gives a reference learner for a task in which the evaluator trains the submission itself on unseen datasets. It keeps the anchor's model family, regularised gradient-boosted trees (Chen & Guestrin, 2016), in the histogram form of LightGBM (Ke et al., 2017), and adds a small budget-aware selection step: four settings of learning rate, leaf count and L2 penalty are tried with early stopping on a 20% holdout (Prechelt, 1998) while half the time budget lasts, and the best one is refitted on all rows. The design leaves ensembling across model families and meta-learned portfolios (Erickson et al., 2020; Feurer et al., 2022) open for entries.

1Introduction

On medium-sized tables, tree ensembles remain the model to beat (Grinsztajn et al., 2022), but their accuracy depends on a few hyperparameters whose best values differ between datasets. When the learner is trained by the evaluator on datasets it has never seen, those choices must be made by the code from the training data alone and within a time budget.

Changes relative to the anchor. Each item states what this submission does differently and where it is in the code.

  1. The anchor's single tuned configuration is replaced by an ordered four-setting grid selected on a holdout within half of the time budget. — model.py: GRID, fit
  2. Histogram split finding with native missing-value routing instead of the anchor's exact and sparsity-aware split search. — model.py: _model
  3. The selected setting is refitted on all rows for 1.25 times its early-stopped number of rounds. — model.py: fit (refit)

2Related Work

Gradient boosting fits an additive model of trees by functional gradient descent (Friedman, 2001); XGBoost adds second-order split gains and L2-regularised leaf values (Chen & Guestrin, 2016), and histogram learners bin features once to make split finding cheap (Ke et al., 2017). Benchmarks on the same suite show that tuned trees outperform neural tabular models at this scale (Grinsztajn et al., 2022).

AutoML systems close the remaining gap by searching configurations and ensembling across model families, either with Bayesian optimisation and meta-learned warm starts (Feurer et al., 2022) or with fixed robust defaults and multi-layer stacking (Erickson et al., 2020). Both assume budgets of minutes to hours per dataset; with one minute of eight cores, which of these ideas still pays off is the open question this task poses.

3Method

3.1Learner

fit() permutes the rows with the given seed and holds out 20%. In a fixed order it fits four HistGradientBoosting settings (learning rate 0.1 with 31 leaves; 0.05 with 15 or 63 leaves and L2 penalty 1; 0.03 with 31 leaves, L2 penalty 10 and 40 rows per leaf), each with internal early stopping (15% validation, patience 30, at most 2,000 rounds) (Pedregosa et al., 2011). It stops trying further settings once half of budget_seconds has passed. The setting with the lowest holdout loss (log-loss or squared error) is refitted on all rows for 1.25 times its early-stopped number of rounds.

3.2Complexity

With N rows, F features, B bins, M rounds and L leaves, binning costs O(NF log N) and each round O(NF + LFB); at most five fits are made per dataset, so the cost is about five times one early-stopped fit.

4Design Rationale

A short ordered grid with early stopping adapts the two most sensitive choices, step size and tree size, at a cost that fits the per-dataset budget even for 10,000 rows and hundreds of features. Refitting on all rows recovers the data spent on the holdout, and scaling the round count by 1.25 accounts for the larger training set.

5Scope and Limitations

One model family cannot use the complementary errors of linear or neural models; the grid is not adapted to dataset size or feature count, and half of the time budget is deliberately left unused.
